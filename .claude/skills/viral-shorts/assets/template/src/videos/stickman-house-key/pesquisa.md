# Fact brief: the house key (pin tumbler lock)

Research done on 2026-11-04 following `references/pesquisa.md` (viral-shorts) and the "present-day facts" step of the Stickman series. Bracketed numbers point to the sources at the end.

**Where and when this applies:** the ordinary metal house key and the pin tumbler cylinder lock used on many front doors today (2020s), especially in North America and Europe. Other lock types exist (lever locks, disc detainer locks, smart locks), and the mix differs by country, so the script never says "every door".

**Verification note:** this container could not open Wikipedia or LockWiki directly (network policy), so the claims below were checked against search-result excerpts from several independent sources. Re-open the sources before publishing commercially.

## Central question
How can a small flat stick of metal with jagged teeth open only its own door?

## How it works, step by step
1. **Parts.** A pin tumbler cylinder has a fixed outer **housing** (shell) and an inner **plug** that can rotate. The key goes into the plug [1][2].
2. **Pin stacks.** Vertical holes are drilled through the housing and the plug. Each hole holds a **spring** on top, a **driver pin**, and a **key pin** at the bottom. Residential cylinders commonly have five or six of these stacks, and the key pins have different lengths [1][3].
3. **Locked.** With no key, the springs push the stacks down, so the driver pins cross the boundary between plug and housing (the **shear line**). That jams the plug and it cannot turn [1][3][4].
4. **The right key.** Each cut on the key lifts its stack to a precise height. With the correct key, the gap between every key pin and driver pin sits exactly on the shear line at the same time, so the plug can turn [1][2][4].
5. **The wrong key.** A wrong key lifts the stacks to the wrong heights, so at least one pin still straddles the shear line and the plug stays stuck [3][4].
6. **Opening.** When the plug turns, a cam or tailpiece at the back moves the bolt or latch and the door can open [1][5].

## Surprising facts (hook candidates)
- The "teeth" are not decoration: each cut depth is a precise height, like one digit of a code [1][4].
- The idea is old. Wooden pin locks are traced back to ancient Egypt, and the modern metal pin tumbler cylinder was developed by Linus Yale Sr. and Linus Yale Jr. in the mid-1800s [1][6]. Sources differ on exact years, so the script avoids dates.
- The pin tumbler is described as one of the most widely used lock designs in the world [2][7]. No source gives hard market-share numbers, so the script does not claim it is "the most common".

## Myths and mistakes
- "The key pushes the door open." No: the key only lets the plug rotate, and the turning plug moves the bolt [1][5].
- "Any key of the right shape fits." Many keys enter the same keyway shape, but only the matching cut depths line up the pins [3][4].
