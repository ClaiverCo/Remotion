# Fact brief: the electric fan

Research done on 2026-11-04 following `references/pesquisa.md` (viral-shorts) and the "present-day facts" step of the Stickman series. Bracketed numbers point to the sources at the end.

**Where and when this applies:** ordinary electric desk, pedestal and ceiling fans used in homes in the 2020s, all over the world. The script only talks about how a fan feels to a person and what it does to room air. It gives no health advice.

## Central question
If a fan does not make the air any colder, why did people in hot rooms feel so much better in front of one?

## How it works, step by step
1. **Motor and blades.** An electric motor turns a set of angled blades. The tilt of each blade pushes air forward as it spins, like a propeller [4][5].
2. **Speed buttons.** The speed setting changes how much power reaches the motor, so it spins slower or faster and moves more or less air [4][5].
3. **The air stays the same temperature.** Blowing air around does not remove heat from it. A fan does not lower the air temperature of a room, and its own motor adds a little heat [2][3][6].
4. **Why people feel cooler.** In still air, a thin layer of warm, humid air builds up next to the skin. The breeze sweeps that layer away, so the body loses heat to the air faster (convection) and sweat evaporates faster. Evaporating sweat carries heat away from the skin [2][3][7].
5. **Cools people, not rooms.** Because the effect happens on the skin (a "wind chill" effect), a fan running in an empty room cools nobody. The U.S. Department of Energy advised turning fans off when you leave a room [1][6].

## Surprising facts (hook candidates)
- A fan in a closed, empty room makes it very slightly *warmer*, because all the electricity it uses ends up as heat [2][6].
- The cooling is "felt", not measured on a thermometer: the air temperature does not change, only how fast the body loses heat [2][3].

## Myths and mistakes
- "A fan cools the room." No: it moves air; it does not take heat out of it [1][2][6].
- "Leave the fan on so the room is cool when you come back." No: it only helps people who are in the breeze [1][6].
- In humid air, sweat evaporates more slowly, so a fan helps less [3]. At very high air temperatures, researchers disagree on how much fans help the body at all [8]. **The script avoids any claim about heatwaves or health.**
